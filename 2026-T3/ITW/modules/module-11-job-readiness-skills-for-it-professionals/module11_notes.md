## 1. Module text

An "IT professional" used to mean a software developer or a support specialist. With information systems and cloud computing everywhere, the profession now covers many occupations: chief technology officer, cybersecurity specialist, product owner, business analyst, cloud engineer and more. The module's aim is a map of those occupations, their responsibilities and their skill sets, so a graduate can choose a specialisation on purpose.

## 2. IT job descriptions (Mallory Careers, 2026)

The page argues that demand for capable IT workers stays strong, and that entry usually depends on skills a candidate can demonstrate, not only on qualifications. The roles it describes, grouped by what they work on:

| Group | Roles | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Analytics engineer, data scientist, database administrator | Build and maintain structured datasets with SQL models and warehouses; analyse large datasets and build predictive models; keep databases secure, fast and correct |
| Software | Software developer, web developer | Write and test applications, systems software, websites and online services |
| Infrastructure | Cloud platform engineer, network engineer, data centre technician, hardware engineer | Build and run cloud infrastructure and automation; design networks; maintain servers; design and test hardware |
| Security | Cyber security specialist | Protect systems and networks; certifications give an advantage |
| Business-facing | Business analyst, systems analyst, IT consultant, IT manager | Examine how an organisation reaches its goals; turn business requirements into IT solutions; advise; lead teams and projects |

## 4. Ten soft skills every IT team needs (Rudder, 2019)

IT leaders name the soft skills they hire and promote for. The article cites a West Monroe study in which 67 percent of HR leaders had withheld a job offer over a candidate's lack of soft skills.

| # | Skill | The point |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Communication | Listening, speaking and writing; "a team that fails to communicate will ultimately fail" |
| 2 | Collaboration | Working with others, more so in remote teams |
| 3 | Ability to learn | Technology changes, so learning never stops |
| 4 | Creativity | Turning a business problem into a technical solution |
| 5 | Healthy ego | Being able to be wrong is part of solving problems |
| 6 | Accountability | Owning the work and the mistakes |
| 7 | Critical thinking | Thinking in an organised, rational way |
| 8 | Empathy | Trust comes from understanding the other person |
| 9 | Flexibility | Projects and tools change mid-way |
| 10 | Passion | What separates great from mediocre people in technology |

## 5. Hard skills vs soft skills (Doyle, 2024)

| | Hard skills | Soft skills |
|---|---|---|
| What | Teachable abilities that are easy to quantify | Subjective people skills that are hard to quantify |
| Learned | In class, from books and training, on the job | Through experience with other people |
| Examples | A degree or certificate, a programming language, a foreign language, machine operation | Communication, flexibility, leadership, problem solving, teamwork, time management, work ethic |
| How to show | List them: resume, certifications, a program you built | Demonstrate them: a concrete example of when you used one |

- Employers find it easier to train a hard skill than a soft one, and increasingly want **hybrid skills**: soft and technical together.
- **Resume:** a skills section, and skills shown inside each job description. **Cover letter:** show the soft skill with a result; do not just claim it. **Interview:** the **STAR** answer: situation, task, action, result.
- Read the job description for the skills wanted. Leave out obsolete tools and skills you do not have.
