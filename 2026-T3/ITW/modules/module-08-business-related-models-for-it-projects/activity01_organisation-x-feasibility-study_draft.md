Wang (2018) says engineers do not just solve problems; they define problems in a way that can be solved. A feasibility study is that definition, written down before Organisation X spends money on integration.

**1. Criteria: is it possible, and is it reasonable?**

- **Technical:** do the current systems expose the data the integrated system needs, and in a usable shape?
- **Economic:** do the benefits outweigh the full cost? Ali (2012) warns that the licence is the smallest part. Training, the learning-curve slowdown and late incompatibilities count too.
- **Operational:** will staff actually use it? Acceptance follows usefulness and ease of use, and people who help design a process are more likely to use it (Degerli & Ozkan, 2014).
- **Schedule:** can it land before the systems it replaces need renewing?

**2. Budgeting options**

Phase the money. Fund a small first integration, then expand only if it proves its value. Coles and Rowley's (1995) decision tree shows why: the smaller start with an option to expand can beat the big build once you weigh the probabilities. Keep the forecast separate from the budget. The forecast is re-stated as you learn; the budget changes only by approval.

**3. Controls and risk**

- A risk register valued as probability x impact, with a contingency sized from it.
- A stage gate before each expansion, with acceptance criteria agreed in advance.
- Sensitivity analysis: would any plausible change in a cost or a probability change the recommendation?
- Change control for scope, so the study stays the baseline.

In my placement the smallest version of this is a spreadsheet the stakeholder checks before anything goes into the portal. It is a cheap first branch that keeps the expensive one optional.

**Question for you:** when the business side wants everything at once (Wang's drone that must fly "as fast as possible" and be "as light as possible"), which criterion do you lead with to get a trade-off decision?
