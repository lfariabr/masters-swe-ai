Privacy compliance

- [Narrator] Many of the laws that impact information security professionals are designed to protect the privacy of individuals and protect individuals against both identity theft and the unwanted disclosure of personal information. In the United States we have a patchwork of laws that affect different industries depending upon the nature of their business and the types of sensitive information that they handle. Probably the most well known of these laws is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act or HIPAA Passed in 1996 HIPAA places strict privacy and security regulations on healthcare providers health insurers and health information clearinghouses. These three groups collectively known as HIPAA covered entities must insure the privacy and security of protected health information or PHI that they handle. HIPAA was updated in 2009 with the passage of the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health or HITECH Act. the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act or FERPA regulates how educational institutions may handle student educational records. FERPA provides students and their parents, if the student is under 18 the right to inspect educational records and request corrections to those records. FERPA also restricts the ways that educational institutions may use and release educational records without the students consent. The Gramm Leach Bliley Act GLBA passed in 1999 covers the financial services sector. GLBA requires that all financial institutions have a written information security program and designate a specific individual who is responsible for information security. GLBA also regulates the ways that financial institutions may share private information even when sharing it with other subsidiaries of the same company. The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act COPPA protects the privacy of children under the age of 13 when they are accessing websites. COPPA requires that websites that knowingly collect information from children have a written privacy policy and provide parents with the ability to review and delete information about their child. COPPA also requires that parents give concent for the collection of information from children under the age of 13 before that collection takes place. One often misunderstood law is the Privacy Act of 1974. The name of this act seems very broad and people often misinterpret it as applying to all organizations. The Privacy Act does restrict the sharing of personal information but it only applies to federal government agencies and has no affect on regulating private individuals or organizations. While the United States has a patchwork of laws that cover very specific use cases the European Union approaches data privacy in a completely different way. The European Union's Data Protections laws cover many different types of personal information in a very broad fashion. EU law requires that organizations handling information about EU citizens follow six principles first personal information must be processed lawfully, fairly and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject. Information must be collected for specific and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a way that's incompatible with those purposes. Information collection must be limited to the minimum information necessary to achieve the specific purposes. And information must be kept accurate and up to date. Information must also be kept in a form which permits the identification of data subjects for no longer than is absolutely necessary. And finally information processing must take place under the responsibility and liability of a designated data controller. European Union law on data privacy is currently in a state of transition. As the EU adopts a new General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR which is scheduled to go into effect in 2018.

- [Teacher] Software is often very valuable intellectual property. Companies invest significant financial and human resources in developing software, and then protect it through the use of license agreements. Software License Agreements describe the terms of use for software that an organization acquires. These agreements contain many different provisions that cover the circumstances of acceptable use. These may include the number and types of individuals who may use the software, the amount of information that may be processed, the locations where data may be processed, and the numbers of servers supporting the software, as well as whatever restrictions the software publisher chooses to include in the agreement. Software license agreements come in many different forms. In some cases, software agreements are detailed contracts that are negotiated between the software vendor and the customer. This is especially true in the case of expensive enterprise software. In these cases, the vendor often proposes a license agreement, and then the custom suggests revisions. And there may be many round of negotiation before the two sides agree on mutually acceptable language. Click-through agreements are on the other end of the spectrum. They're take it or leave it agreements where the customer simply clicks an I agree button as they purchase software or sign up for a service. These click-through agreements can be dangerous because users typically don't read them carefully, if at all, and they may be committing their organization to undesirable restrictions. You should also be familiar with the term shrink-wrap agreements on the exam. Shrink-wrap agreements are essentially the same as click-through agreements, but are included on the physical package for software and take effect when the user opens the shrink-wrap or breaks a seal on the software.
