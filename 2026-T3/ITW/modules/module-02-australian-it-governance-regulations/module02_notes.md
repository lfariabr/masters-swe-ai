### 2. Samuelson, P. (2000). Five challenges for regulating the global information society.

**Citation:** Samuelson, P. (2000). *Five challenges for regulating the global information society*. SSRN. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=234743

**Purpose:** A Berkeley law professor sets out the five policy challenges of regulating the internet, using US and EU examples. Local copy: [r2_five-challenges-global-information-society.pdf](r2_five-challenges-global-information-society.pdf).

---

#### 1. The five challenges
| # | Challenge | Example in the paper |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Old law or new law?** Apply, adapt or replace existing law | Streaming (broadcaster or not?), antitrust in *US v. Microsoft*, copyright adapting to digital |
| 2 | **Proportional response** when new law is needed | The Communications Decency Act was struck down as overbroad (*Reno v. ACLU*); the US sees EU database and data directives as overprotective |
| 3 | **Flexible laws** for fast-changing technology | Laws should be "predictable, minimalist, consistent, and simple" and **technology-neutral**: UETA (simple) vs UCITA (complex) |
| 4 | **Preserve human values** under economic and technical pressure | Privacy versus cheap data mining and cookies; encryption as speech (*Bernstein*); fair use under the DMCA |
| 5 | **Coordinate across nations** | The US-EU conflict over personal data flows |

#### 2. Core argument
- Regulating the internet is about building an **information society** that keeps social and cultural values, not only about infrastructure and economics.
- **Minimalist first:** add regulation only when experience shows it is needed.

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. Challenge 3 explains why my school's **internal governance** (who signs off which data) matters more day to day than statute: the law lags the tools.
2. Technology-neutral thinking applies to my proposal too: describe the control ("validate before publish"), not a vendor product.

### 3. Chapple, M. (2021). CISSP cert prep: Security and risk management (compliance videos).

**Citation:** Chapple, M. (2021, January 21). *CISSP cert prep: 1 Security and risk management* [Videos]. LinkedIn Learning.

**Purpose:** Three short videos on the compliance obligations a security professional faces. Transcript and notes: [r3_security-risk-privacy-compliance-transcript.md](r3_security-risk-privacy-compliance-transcript.md). The capture covers the privacy video in full; the legislative and licensing videos are cut off mid-sentence.

---

#### 1. Four sources of compliance obligation
| Type | Made by | Enforcement |
|---|---|---|
| **Criminal law** | Legislature | Prison or probation (the only type that removes liberty) |
| **Civil law** | Legislature | Damages, court orders |
| **Administrative law** | Executive agencies | Detailed regulations filling gaps in statute |
| **Private regulation** | Industry bodies | **By contract** (e.g. PCI DSS) |

#### 2. Privacy regimes
- **US patchwork:** HIPAA (health), FERPA (student records), GLBA (finance), COPPA (children under 13), Privacy Act 1974 (federal agencies only).
- **EU GDPR** (since 25 May 2018), one broad regime. Article 5 sets **seven principles**:
  1. lawfulness, fairness and transparency
  2. purpose limitation
  3. data minimisation
  4. accuracy
  5. storage limitation
  6. integrity and confidentiality
  7. accountability
- The "six principles" framing in the video is historical.

#### 3. Software licensing
- **Negotiated** (enterprise contracts) · **click-through** (take it or leave it) · **shrink-wrap** (legacy physical media).

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. FERPA is the US analogue of my context. In Australia the equivalent duties sit in the Privacy Act and the APPs.
2. **Data minimisation** and **accuracy** map directly to A1: "read-only, only what the feature needs" and "fresh is not complete" (APP 10).

### 4. Mishra, N. (2019). Building bridges: International trade law, internet governance, and the regulation of data flows.

**Citation:** Mishra, N. (2019). Building bridges: International trade law, internet governance, and the regulation of data flows. *Vanderbilt Journal of Transnational Law, 52*(2), 463-509.

**Purpose:** Links internet governance to WTO and preferential trade law through three principles, and argues that privacy and security enable open data flows. Local copy: [r4_trade-law-internet-governance-data-flows.pdf](r4_trade-law-internet-governance-data-flows.pdf).

---

#### 1. Three internet-governance principles
- **Openness · Security · Privacy.** When they are implemented "in a reasoned and transparent manner", security and privacy **enable** openness. This challenges the view that they only restrict data flows.

#### 2. Why trade law needs them
- **Different regimes:** trade law is binding and state-to-state (WTO, PTAs), while internet governance is multistakeholder and non-binding (IETF, IGF).
- **Out of date:** GATS predates the digital economy. Recent PTA e-commerce chapters are more informed.
- **Use in disputes:** the three principles can act as technical or factual evidence when judging **data-restrictive measures** such as data localisation.

#### 3. Recommendation
- Build dialogue between the trade and internet communities (WTO Public Forum, IGF sessions). Multistakeholder bodies fix technical risks faster than trade negotiations can.

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. At school scale, a secure, privacy-respecting system lets the school **share more** with families and staff, not less.
2. This gives A1 an argument that the de-identified, staff-reviewed scope enables the project. It is not a limitation to apologise for.
