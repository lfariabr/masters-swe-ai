## Key Highlights

### 1. Boughton, C. (2013). What is an ICT professional anyway?

**Citation:** Boughton, C. (2013). What is an ICT professional anyway? In J. Weckert & R. Lucas (Eds.), *Professionalism in the information and communication technology industry* (pp. 77-94). ANU E Press.

**Purpose:** An experienced software engineer asks what makes someone an ICT professional. He uses informal "types" of ICT people, a set of professionalism criteria and the traits of a profession, and concludes that ICT is still a semi-profession.

---

#### 1. Six characteristics of individual professionalism
- **Competency · ethicity · knowledgeability · learning ability · care · pride.**
- Missing even one means the person is not a professional, even if they use the label (p. 78).

#### 2. The ICT "types" (informal, not authoritative)
| Type | Strength | Weak spot |
|---|---|---|
| Highly broadly technical, and you know it | Competent, ethical, thrives under pressure | Poor team player, not diplomatic |
| Highly broadly technical, and you don't know it | Quiet, diligent, ethical | Hates pressure, won't volunteer expertise |
| Highly narrowly technical (know it / don't) | One technique known very well | Uses it for every problem, resists new ideas |
| Big-picture broadly technical | Visionary, good at analysis, architecture and design | Weak on verification and validation; may ignore wider stakeholders |
| Big-picture narrowly technical | Tries to emulate the visionary | Same limited solution every time; hides what they don't know |
| Concepts only | Focus on human use | Very limited technical knowledge |
| I can do it | Enthusiastic, smart new graduate | Over-confident; needs direction |
| I can't do it | Can change in the right environment | Lacks confidence and initiative |
| Other professional | Expert in another field | Does ICT work or makes ICT decisions without competence or advice |

- 🔑 **Use:** the types are a lens for spotting professionalism gaps, not personality labels (p. 77).

#### 3. Professional vs profession
- **Criteria for a professional (Table 1):** training, intellectualism, autonomy, judgement, independence, service, dedication, pride, honest and trustworthy. Maister adds that professionalism is "predominantly an attitude".
- **Traits of a profession (Table 4):** exclusive control of a body of knowledge (BOK), autonomy of practice, norm of altruism, authority over clients, distinctive culture, recognition.
- **Verdict (Table 6):** ICT is a **semi-profession**, like project management. It has contributing BOKs (SWEBOK, ACS Core BOK) but no single ICT BOK, and weak enforcement of codes of ethics.
- **ACS Core BOK, mandatory areas:** ethics and professional practice, interpersonal communications, and project management with quality assurance.

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. Activity 3 asks for an example of each type. The self-check is to name the type that fits my placement work and the traits I need to borrow.
2. The ACS mandatory areas (ethics, communication, PM) are the same areas covered in Modules 3, 4 and 7.
3. A1 Conclusion: one honest lesson about myself. "Big-picture" framing without verification detail is a known weak spot in the chapter.

### 2. Taylor Institute for Teaching and Learning. (2019). A quality framework for work-integrated learning.

**Citation:** Taylor Institute for Teaching and Learning. (2019, June 1). *A quality framework for work-integrated learning (2019)* [Video]. YouTube. (Framework: McRae, Pretti & Church, 2018.)

**Purpose:** A University of Waterloo presenter defines WIL and sets out the CEWIL "Triple-A" quality framework across five stakeholders. Transcript: [r2_wil-quality-framework-video.md](r2_wil-quality-framework-video.md).

---

#### 1. What WIL is
- **WIL** is a subset of experiential learning: an intentional partnership of institution, student and workplace.
- **Nine CEWIL types:** apprenticeship, co-op, internship, entrepreneurship, service-learning, applied research, practicum or clinical placement, field placement, work experience.
- **Why quality matters:** calls for every graduate to do WIL risk "activity for activity's sake" unless learning outcomes are defined.

#### 2. Aims of the five stakeholders
| Stakeholder | Typical aims |
|---|---|
| Student | Hands-on learning, applying theory, employability, career clarity, networks, income |
| Host organisation | Talent pipeline, new ideas and innovation, staff supervision capacity, work done |
| Educator | Engagement, skills, relevance fed back into curriculum |
| Institution | Recruitment, retention, community engagement |
| Government | Skills gap, employability, return on funding |

#### 3. Actions (PEAR, before / during / after)
- **P**edagogy · **E**xperience · **A**ssessment · **R**eflection: the four pillars of high-impact WIL.
- **Experience:** students and hosts must be identified, vetted and prepared; hosts act as educator partners, not "cheap labour".
- **Assessment:** assessing learning, not just "showed up and didn't break anything".
- **Reflection:** "experience without reflection doesn't really lead to learning". It is the hardest pillar and the most often skipped.

#### 4. Achievements + continuous improvement
- Each stakeholder measures success against its aims, and the loop feeds back into the aims (quality improvement).

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. All three e-Journals are reflection pieces, so PEAR's "R" is what gets graded.
2. My placement has to satisfy several aims at once: mine (skill growth), the school's (a system leaders can evaluate) and the university's (assessable learning).
3. "Before" actions such as the Letter of Support and agreed scope are part of quality, not paperwork.
