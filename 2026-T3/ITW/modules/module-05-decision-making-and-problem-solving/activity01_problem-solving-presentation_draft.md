I would build the presentation around my placement project, a staff view of student academic results at a school, and follow the eight-step process (RedVectorOnline, 2016) so stakeholders can see how we got from the problem to the decision.

1. **The problem, in hard terms.** Not "the data is messy", which says nothing (Samuel & Weir, 1999). Instead: award rankings must follow agreed rules, and one report view was missing some rows for a semester.
2. **What we learned before choosing.** The data sources we mapped, the gaps we found and the rules the academic lead confirmed in writing. These are the facts the plan stands on.
3. **The need behind the request.** The brief arrived as a solution: "build a profile page". Asking what staff really need gave us a better target: one view they can trust, with rules applied the same way every time.
4. **Three options, compared on one slide.** Read the existing tables first, build a new data layer first, or keep the manual workbook. I would score each on effectiveness, feasibility and impact, then explain why we chose the first: staff get value early, and the risk of change stays low.
5. **How we check and repeat it.** Results are checked against what teachers already see, and the rules note becomes the standard procedure.

For the visuals, I would draw the data flow in 5 to 13 boxes (Wujec, 2013), and ideally build it with the stakeholders, not just present it to them.

**Question for you:** when you compare options, how do you stop the one you already prefer from winning by default?
