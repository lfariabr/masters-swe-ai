## Key Highlights

### 1. Ali, A. & Eagar, M. (2022). Interview with Mark Eagar.

**Citation:** Ali, A. & Eagar, M. (2022). *Interview with Mark Eagar* [Podcast]. Torrens University Australia.

**Purpose:** An engineering strategist gives early-career advice: the mindset shift from study to work, certifications, languages and workplace politics. Transcript: [r1_mark-eagar-podcast-transcript.md](r1_mark-eagar-podcast-transcript.md).

---

#### 1. From academic to operational mindset
- **You are there to learn.** A new graduate is not there to apply everything from university. Work ethic and fast learning carry the early years.
- **Working with people is "the most absolute crucial thing".** Every environment is different, and people skills decide progression to PM, senior manager and director.
- **Small vs corporate:** small firms give breadth (jack of all trades), corporates give one narrow role but recognisable names. His advice is to alternate between the two to get the best of both.

#### 2. Certifications and degrees as an "index"
- A degree or certification builds a **mental index**, a reference framework. "You don't need to know the answer, but you need to know where to go and look."
- **Perceived needs vs action needs:** start by addressing what the client *thinks* the need is.

#### 3. Technology views (opinion, not evidence)
- He stresses object-oriented fundamentals (C++, then C#, Java, Python) and calls Python the language for AI. He calls Microsoft the dominant enterprise environment.
- His forecasts on XR, holograms, laptops becoming obsolete and Bitcoin replacing banks are personal predictions with no sources. Treat them critically.

#### 4. What he wished he had known
- **Meet the perceived problem first:** even when you see the answer, deliver on the client's own framing first, then earn the right to propose a step change.
- **Learn the politics:** results come through understanding the political landscape. Promotion often follows loyalty.

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. "Address the perceived need first" is the relationship lesson in my A1 Observations: deliver what the academic staff asked for, then propose the foundation fix.
2. The "index" idea gives the A3 reflection an honest framing: the degree taught me where to look, the placement taught me how to scope.
3. Read critically: the advice on people is solid, but the tech predictions are unsupported, and "people are stupid" is a framing to avoid in professional writing.

### 2. Taylor, J. (2004). Chapter 7: Risk management in information technology projects.

**Citation:** Taylor, J. (2004). *Managing information technology projects*. AMACOM. Ch. 7, pp. 152-182.

**Purpose:** Defines risk and gives an eight-step risk management model, built on the PMBOK, with tools for identifying, filtering, ranking, quantifying and responding to IT project risk. Local copy: [r2_taylor-ch7-risk-management-it-projects.pdf](r2_taylor-ch7-risk-management-it-projects.pdf).

---

#### 1. Risk defined
- **Three components:** the **event**, its **probability** and its **impact**, good or bad (p. 153).
- **Business risk:** loss *or* gain, for example a scope change. Manage it.
- **Pure (insurable) risk:** loss only, for example natural disasters, or taking on a project because "most" requirements are within capability. Avoid it or transfer it; never try to manage it (pp. 153-154).
- Opening line: *project management is conflict management* (p. 152).

#### 2. The eight-step model (Exhibit 7-1)
```
1 Plan -> 2 Identify & assess -> 3 Qualify -> 4 Quantify -> 5 Respond
   ^                                                            |
   8 Document & archive <- 7 Control <- 6 Track  <--------------+
              (continual evaluation wraps every step)
```

| Step | Key tools / ideas |
|---|---|
| **1 Plan** | Starts at project selection. Inputs: charter, policies, contracts or SOW, **WBS** (the most important tool), network analysis (**path convergence** risk). Output: **risk management plan**, 9 sections (Exhibit 7-3) |
| **2 Identify** | Team **brainstorming**, **Ishikawa/fishbone**, checklists (warning: false security), **assumption analysis**. No filtering yet |
| **3 Qualify** | **Filter**: in scope? affects fit, form or function? significant? likely? early? If not early, put it on a watch list. Map low/med/high to % (very low 0-5% … very high 81-100%) |
| | **Prioritise**: manage about **10 risks at a time**. Use comparative pairwise ranking with weights for the organisation's risk appetite |
| **4 Quantify** | **Expected value** `EV = Σ pᵢ·Xᵢ`, **NPV** for trade-offs (bus-fleet example: Model A wins once time value is counted), **decision trees** (R&D example, EV $22.9M vs $23M license) |
| **5 Respond** | **Avoid · Transfer · Mitigate · Accept**, plus **triggers** |
| **6 Track** | Is the response working? New risks? Are the assumptions still valid? |
| **7 Control** | **Contingency plans** (planned, PM-controlled reserve) vs **workarounds** (unforeseen, from a **management reserve** held by senior management) |
| **8 Archive** | **Lessons learned**: baseline vs actual, issues, identified and unidentified risks, what went right or wrong |

#### 3. Risk management plan outline (Exhibit 7-3)
1. Name and scope
2. Methodology
3. Roles and responsibilities
4. Funding (contingency)
5. Measurement and interpretation
6. Levels of response responsibility (thresholds for escalation)
7. Communication plan
8. Tracking and documentation
9. Appendixes: risk table and risk response plan

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. **Escalation thresholds (section 6)** are what FNB lacked in R3: the PM had no route to escalate resource risk.
2. **Assumption analysis** fits my placement. The assumption "fresh data = complete data" failed (the Trial-marks gap), which is an assumption risk, not a technical bug.
3. **For A2 or A3:** the eight steps give a ready structure for the risk section of the project synopsis. Keep a top-10 risk table and a watch list.

### 3. Kerzner, H. R. (2013). MIS Project Management at First National Bank.

**Citation:** Kerzner, H. R. (2013). *Project management case studies* (4th ed.). Wiley, pp. 72-85.

**Purpose:** A bank with a formal proposal-and-priority process still fails a top-priority corporate database project. The case shows how a proposal process works and how people and structure defeat it. Full paraphrased summary: [r3_kerzner-mis-pm-first-national-bank.md](r3_kerzner-mis-pm-first-national-bank.md).

---

#### 1. The process (what good looks like on paper)
- The **Priorities Committee** of division heads is the only route to IS funding.
- **Flow:** problem statement, then impact statement, then committee, then full **project proposal**, then committee ranking.
- **The proposal covers:**
  - the problem
  - alternatives
  - impact on each division
  - costs
  - schedule
  - cost-benefit
  - long-range implications
  - a recommendation
- **Other controls:**
  - "moving window" estimating (detail for the next subphase only)
  - four refunding gates
  - design freezes
  - **ten quality reviews** by a Quality Review Board

#### 2. Why the Corporate Database failed
| Failure | Evidence in the case |
|---|---|
| **Single-voice requirements** | The proposal was built almost entirely from one sponsor; others deferred: "That project is John's baby" (p. 82) |
| **No users on the team** | All team members came from ISD (p. 81) |
| **Rushed plan** | The sponsor called planning meetings a waste, and the GM pushed for speed |
| **Under-resourcing** | Four analysts were requested and two juniors given; the GM did not respond |
| **Resistance to change** | Divisions feared losing functions to Corporate Marketing and cancelled meetings |
| **No escalation** | The PM stayed silent before his own appraisal |
| **Outcome** | More than 90% of tasks were redone, the PM was replaced, the second PM quit, the sponsor retired, the project was tabled |

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. **Process does not replace stakeholders.** Validate requirements with every affected group, not only the requester.
2. The **proposal sections** are a ready skeleton for A2's project synopsis: problem, alternatives, impact, cost-benefit, recommendation.
3. **Ethics link to M3:** knowing the work is substandard and not escalating is a professional failure, not just a PM one.

### 4. ProjectManager. (2014). Project proposal writing: How to write a winning project proposal.

**Citation:** ProjectManager. (2014, May 19). *Project proposal writing: How to write a winning project proposal* [Video]. YouTube. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jsGBuu88WE0

**Purpose:** A seven-minute guide to the structure and selling of a project proposal. Transcript: [r4_project-proposal-writing-video-transcript.md](r4_project-proposal-writing-video-transcript.md).

---

#### 1. "People buy from people"
- The document alone won't get funding. The sponsor and champions must **lobby decision makers** in one-on-ones, and use that feedback to sharpen the proposal.
- Decision makers decide to "thrill or kill" within **about five minutes**.

#### 2. Proposal sections, in order
| Section | What it must do |
|---|---|
| **Problem statement** | Paint a bleak picture with **specific missed opportunities or incurred costs**. Avoid vague "efficiency" claims |
| **Vision** | Tie the project to the organisation's long-range strategy, or it looks like a "rogue project" |
| **Benefits** | Specific and **measurable** capabilities gained or costs avoided |
| **Deliverables** | The artefacts users will actually receive |
| **Success criteria** | **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound |
| **Approach** | Deadlines, plan, vendors vs internal staff, agile vs waterfall |
| **Cost / budget** | Funding plan against deliverables |
| **Executive summary** | Add one if the early sections run past about two pages |

- **Flow like a story:** every deliverable must trace back to a stated problem or benefit, with no new topics introduced mid-document.

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. My A1 problem statement already has a specific incident (Trial marks missing from the warehouse). That is the "specific example" the video asks for.
2. "Tie to strategy" maps to the Letter of Support wording: a learning analytics capability for student support.

### 5. Invensis Learning. (2021). Project Management 101.

**Citation:** Invensis Learning. (2021, February 16). *Project management 101: Project management tutorial for beginners - Project management fundamentals* [Video]. YouTube. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cLXkOYaZ_K0

**Purpose:** A one-hour primer on PM history, the life cycle, the PMBOK knowledge areas, methodologies, tools and certifications. Transcript: [r5_project-management-101-video-transcript.md](r5_project-management-101-video-transcript.md).

---

#### 1. Timeline highlights
- **1950s:** Gantt charts (Hoover Dam); **CPM** (DuPont, 1957); **PERT** (US Navy Polaris, 1958).
- **1969-1987:** PMI founded (1969); **Brooks's law**, "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" (1975); PMBOK 1st edition (1987).
- **1986-2001:** Scrum (1986); PRINCE (1989), then PRINCE2 (1996); CCPM (1997); **Agile Manifesto** (2001).

#### 2. Life cycle (5 phases) and the 10 PMBOK knowledge areas
```
Initiation (charter, feasibility) -> Planning (plan, WBS, schedule) -> Execution
      -> Monitoring & Control (cost / time / scope triangle) -> Closure (handover, retro)
```
- **Knowledge areas:** integration, scope, schedule, cost, quality, resource, communication, risk, procurement, stakeholder. These match the PMI (2018) list in `notes.md`.

#### 3. Methodologies: when to choose which
| Method | Choose when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| **Waterfall** | Fixed goals, fixed budget, little stakeholder input | Any chance requirements change |
| **Agile** | Unclear or evolving requirements, high collaboration | Budget can't absorb change, very rigid deadlines |
| **Scrum** | Sprints (1-4 weeks); ceremonies: planning, daily, review, retro | |
| **Kanban** | Visual flow, **WIP limits** against bottlenecks | Highly complex projects |
| **Scrumban** | Scrum cadence plus Kanban pull and WIP | |
| **PRINCE2** | 7 principles (e.g. continued business justification, manage by stages and by exception) | Project doesn't fit the principles |
| **CPM / CCPM** | Many dependencies; CCPM adds buffers | Durations unknown |
| **Lean** | Cut waste (muda, mura, muri) | Implementing it costs more than the waste |
| **XP** | Software teams: simplicity, communication, feedback, respect, courage | Team won't follow the rules |
| **Outcome mapping** | Behavioural change and long-term impact (non-profit) | Measurable deliverables needed |

- **Selection factors:** team size, budget, flexibility, timeline, stakeholder collaboration, risk, resources, scalability, resistance to change.

#### 4. Tools and certifications
- **Tools:** ProofHub, Trello, Wrike, **Jira**, Workzone, Asana, Basecamp, Bitrix24, Slack, GanttPRO. **Common features:** planning, scheduling, collaboration, risk, time and budget tracking.
- **Certifications:** **PMP** (PMI, advanced; needs a degree plus 36 months leading projects plus 35 hrs education), **PRINCE2** Foundation and Practitioner (AXELOS), **CAPM** (PMI, entry level), **CSM** (Scrum Alliance).
- The salary and job-growth figures are 2021 claims from the video and are not verified here.

#### Key Takeaways for ITW601
1. My placement work runs **Kanban-like**: helpdesk tickets pulled as they arrive, with Git release notes as the closure record. Naming this accurately helps the A1 "planning tools" bullet.
2. Brooks's law and the CPM/CCPM buffers explain FNB (R3): adding three seniors late did not save the CDB.
3. CAPM is the realistic credential at my stage. PMP needs 36 months of leading projects.
