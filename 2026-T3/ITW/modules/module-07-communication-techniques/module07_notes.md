## Key Highlights

### 1. Communicating content in the right context

**Citations:**
- Eraut, M. (2004). Informal learning in the workplace. *Studies in Continuing Education, 26*(2), 247-273. https://doi.org/10.1080/158037042000225245
- Ferguson, E. S. (1977). The mind's eye: Nonverbal thought in technology. *Science, 197*(4306), 827-836. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.197.4306.827

**Purpose (from the module text):** engineers communicate to solve a problem, so content usually beats context. The module argues that in a multidisciplinary workplace the context now matters as much, and that visual representation is the bridge between the two.

**Themes:**

1. **Content first is a habit, not a rule.** Engineering communication tends to present the solution. That works when the listener shares the problem frame, and fails when they don't.
2. **Three systems of representation.** Mental (what the speaker pictures), visual (diagrams, sketches, screens) and linguistic (text and speech). Communication is complete when all three line up. Ferguson (1977) showed that much of engineering thought is nonverbal, so forcing it into prose alone loses information.
3. **Informal communication carries most of the learning.** Eraut (2004) found that workplace learning happens mostly through informal channels: asking, observing, working alongside. Formal documents matter, but the corridor conversation is where understanding is built.
4. **Diversity and stakeholder expectation.** As teams become multidisciplinary, the speaker has to translate across professional vocabularies. The same number means different things to a teacher, a finance officer and a developer.

**Key takeaways for ITW601:**
- The awards rules note worked because it was a table and a worked example, not a paragraph. The academic stakeholder corrected one rule within a day because she could see the rule applied to a student she knew.
- The meeting pack and the cue card for the 30 September meeting were visual representation in Ferguson's sense: one page the stakeholder could point at.
- Most of the rule discoveries (English floor, latest-result counts, accelerants) came from informal exchanges: an email thread, a call, a question at a desk. Eraut's point, lived.
- A2's interview framing (the review of the awards page) is a context exercise. The stakeholder's frame is fairness to students; mine is correctness of the build. The page has to speak to hers.

### 2. Cerri's seven-step communication process

**Citation:** Cerri, S. (2000). Effective communication skills for engineers. *Proceedings of the 2000 IEEE Engineering Management Society*, 625-629. https://doi.org/10.1109/EMS.2000.872578

**Purpose (from the module text):** a repeatable strategy for communication in any setting, written for engineers who are moving into roles where persuading matters as much as solving.

**The seven steps, as the module lists them:**

| Step | What it says | Plain version |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Understand the verbal and non-verbal requirements of the setting; identify the mode the listener needs | Pick the channel and register before the content |
| 2 | Mirror or match, pace, then lead | Start from how they communicate, then move them |
| 3 | Understand complex mapping: their outlook, knowledge and experience | Know what the listener already believes about the problem |
| 4 | Send the message | Say it; expect it may land badly |
| 5 | Check that the message was received and understood; use visual and non-visual cues | Ask, watch, confirm |
| 6 | If not received, review steps 1-3 and re-send | Change the framing, not the volume |
| 7 | If received, send the next message | One message at a time |

**Themes:**

1. **Steps 1-3 are preparation.** More than half the process happens before anything is said. That's the engineering instinct applied to communication: understand the system before changing it.
2. **Step 5 is the one engineers skip.** Sending is not communicating. The check is where the listener's frame shows up.
3. **Mirroring is not mimicry.** It means matching vocabulary, pace and format so the listener spends no effort on translation, then leading them to the new idea.
4. **The loop is iterative.** A message that fails goes back to step 1, not to step 4 with more detail.

**Key takeaways for ITW601:**
- The 23 October Year 11 email followed steps 1-4 well (her vocabulary, her format from Year 12, one question at the end) and skipped step 5. Her reply the following week showed two misunderstandings: the "all clear" was provisional, and "like the one you did for Year 12" meant a wider spreadsheet than I had read it to mean. A call after sending would have caught both.
- Step 2 in practice: the stakeholder thinks in spreadsheets, so every deliverable is a workbook before it is a page. The portal page comes after she has accepted the workbook, not instead of it.
- Step 3 in practice: the ICT sponsor's frame is risk and ownership (who owns a table after cutover), the stakeholder's is fairness, the coordinator's is release timing. The same awards build is three different messages.
- For A2: the managerial process plan already has a weekly checkpoint and written rule confirmation. Cerri gives the vocabulary to explain why those exist.
