For the last visit to Organisation X, I would treat reporting as the evidence trail of the contracts. A report is only as good as the agreement it reports against.

**1. Contractual responsibilities**

*External:*
- **Vendors and integrators.** Specifications with measurable targets, acceptance tests, service levels, and a variation process for change. Smith (2003) warns that handing scope to a contractor hands over control, so the client keeps the acceptance test.
- **External QA specialists.** A statement of work that says what and when, not how, with responsibility matched by authority (Blanchard & Blyler, 2016).
- **Clients and the regulator.** Privacy, record keeping and audit obligations for financial data. These are not negotiable, so they belong in every downstream contract.

*Internal:*
- **Sponsor and project engineer.** Who authorises the project, owns the baseline and signs off each release.
- **Business owners.** Who owns each data set and each requirement.

**2. Overseeing and mapping internal controls**

- **Map every requirement to an owner, a metric and a verification method.** The table is the control map.
- **Separate verification from validation.** De Weck (MIT OpenCourseWare, 2017) shows a system can meet every written requirement and still fail its stakeholders. Testing reconciliations against the specification is verification. Staff running real month-end cycles in a pilot is validation.
- **Baseline, then control change.** Once requirements are agreed, every change states its impact and goes to a change board (Blanchard & Blyler, 2016).
- **Report on a fixed rhythm.** Keep an issues register, close issues promptly and record the decisions (Walker & Rowlinson, 2008).

In my placement there is no formal contract. The signed-off rules note plays that role: each rule change goes back to the person who owns the rules, and access is tested for every role on the server.

**Question for you:** should the external QA specialists sign off validation, or only verification?
