## Key Highlights

### 1. The module text: contract, specification, control, accountability

- **The contract records what the customer wants and what the provider can deliver.** Specifications must be correct translations of the customer's requirements, qualitative and quantitative.
- **Contract review runs for the whole period of performance**, because requirements change:
  - balance requirements against capability, resources and regulation;
  - review jointly once the contract is drafted;
  - review performance with the customer periodically;
  - record customer satisfaction.
- **Specifications have a target and limits.** Run or control charts show the range. That **verifies** the process. **Validation** only happens when the process is trialled against the specification and every measure is recorded.
- **Internal control** gives assurance on three things: effective operations, credible records and reports, and compliance.
- **Control elements of a process** (Stimson's Figure 2): equipment, documented procedures, skills, and **measures of effectiveness (MOE)** that verify and validate both process and product.
- **Change:** the structure should detect change in the input, the environment or the process, and the controls for it must be documented and kept current.
- **Responsibility, authority, accountability.** Authority is ownership of the resources for a task. Control comes from defining the task and its **metric** precisely, then assigning it.

**Key takeaway for ITW601:** the placement has no formal contract, but it has every piece of one.
- The agreement: the written rules note and R1-R4.
- The contract review: the academic lead's review rounds (one correction on 30 Sep, two rules added on 9 Oct, one decision on 30 Oct).
- The MOE: the zero-difference check.
- The accountability line: the academic lead owns the rules; the ICT sponsor owns the decision to enable anything for staff.

### 2. Contracts define who does what

**Citation:** Smith, N. (2003). Roles and responsibilities in project contract management. In J. R. Turner (Ed.), *Contracting for project management* (pp. 1-17). Routledge.

**Purpose:** Smith sets out the roles needed across the project supply chain and the contract types that supply them (pp. 1-4). He argues the contract is the mechanism that creates the project organisation and defines its commercial nature (p. 1).

**Themes:**

1. **The contract builds the organisation.** No organisation has every skill a project needs, so the rest is bought in (p. 1). The contract creates the project organisation, employs project managers and procures goods and services (p. 1).
2. **Three traditional roles.** The client initiates and sanctions, the consultant designs, and the contractor implements (p. 2). The client's project manager carries responsibility for all phases (p. 6). A body such as the Board must authorise the project and set the manager's terms of reference (p. 6).
3. **Objectives come before strategy.** Time, cost and performance all matter, but one is likely to dominate (p. 6). "Only if the project purpose and objectives can be clearly defined can the most appropriate procurement strategy be identified" (p. 6). Objectives can shift, so constant monitoring is needed (p. 6).
4. **Transferring scope transfers control.** Integrated strategies remove the design-construction interface, but the client loses control over much of the life cycle (p. 8). Under turnkey, change outside a formal contract variation is "almost non-existent" (p. 10).
5. **Trust replaces privity.** In management contracting the client has no direct contract with works contractors (p. 12). Trust and common commitment between the project manager and management contractor become critical (p. 12).
6. **Clear roles and early dispute handling.** The 1995 ECC split the engineer into designer, supervisor and project manager, plus an external adjudicator (p. 15). Disputes left unresolved erode trust (p. 13). Resolution climbs a six-step ladder from prevention to litigation (p. 14).

| Strategy | Who carries responsibility | Client trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional (pp. 2-7) | Separate consultant and contractor | Contractor joins late |
| Design and build (p. 8) | Contractor does detailed design and build | Needs a precise client specification |
| Turnkey (pp. 8-10) | Contractor designs, builds and commissions | Change only by formal variation |
| Concession / FBOOT (p. 10) | Promoter covers almost the whole life cycle | Promoter must raise finance |
| Management contract (pp. 10-12) | Management contractor runs work packages | No privity, so trust matters |

**Key takeaways for ITW601:**
- There is no formal contract here, but the rules note and R1-R4 play the same role. They work like a turnkey performance specification: R1 is the acceptance test, and any rules change goes back through the academic lead as a formal variation.
- Smith's split of roles fits the team. The ICT sponsor authorises the work and sets terms of reference. The academic lead owns the rules, like the designer. The independent review before merge acts as the supervisor. AI coding agents work like subcontractors, while responsibility for the result stays with the developer.
- Use the bottom of the dispute ladder first. Prevention means clear rules and server-side access tests for R3. Negotiation means raising a workbook difference with the academic lead early, before it erodes trust.

### 3. Procurement choice, relationships and measured outcomes

**Citation:** Walker, D. H. T., & Rowlinson, S. (Eds.). (2008). *Procurement systems: A cross-industry project management perspective*. Taylor & Francis.

**Chapters read:** Ch2, Ch9, Ch13. Ch6 and Ch14 are not in the supplied file.

**Purpose:** The book treats procurement as more than a contract. The contract is one part of a wider system that also covers culture, trust and governance. Project type should drive how work is bought and controlled.

**Themes:**

1. **Ch2 - Project type drives procurement choice.** Turner and Cochrane's matrix sorts projects by how well goals and methods are defined (pp. 36-37). A "one-size-fits-all" approach is unwise (p. 41). Where goals are clear but methods are not, the aim can be to "specify a fixed outcome (or set of outcomes) while leaving delivery methods flexible" (p. 37).

2. **Ch2 - The contract is only part of the system.** Figure 2.2 places options on two axes: how relational the parties are and how integrated design and delivery are (p. 42). The contract strategy "is not the procurement system but only a part of it" (p. 44). A good brief sets the "rules of the game", including how changes are handled (p. 57). Contract administration needs clearly stated standards and practical measures (p. 60).

3. **Ch2 - Relational forms reduce formal control.** Trust lowers the need to monitor each other and to rely on formal controls (p. 53). Partnering adds an agreement on top of a traditional contract. Alliancing shares pain and gain, so parties "sink or swim together" (pp. 53-54).

4. **Ch9 - Culture makes or breaks relational contracting.** Task cultures suit projects. Role cultures are a barrier to change (p. 295). Too many rules can slow decisions (pp. 296-297). Commitment is needed at every level, and failing to follow up or close issues leads to failure (p. 302).

5. **Ch13 - Alliances innovate, but innovation must be captured.** Alliances use open-book accounting and "best-for-project" decisions. This avoids the paper chase of variation claims (p. 404). In the case alliance, the work finished six months early and ten percent under budget (p. 409). An innovation register, a value-management register, a project learning system and a completion report captured what was learned (pp. 415-416). The authors still found the process ad hoc (p. 416).

| Turner and Cochrane type (pp. 36-37) | Goals | Methods | Procurement emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engineering ("earth") | Defined | Defined | Detailed specification and control |
| Project development ("water") | Defined | Not defined | Fix outcomes, leave methods flexible |
| Applications software ("fire") | Not defined | Defined | Require a known method, keep goals fluid |
| Research/change ("air") | Not defined | Not defined | Phase the work or link intangible to tangible outcomes |

**Key takeaways for ITW601:**
- The awards rebuild is a "water" project. R1-R4 fix the outcomes (zero differences, a reason for every unranked student, server-side role access, source and run date on every view). Methods stay open, including AI coding agents, with independent review as the control.
- There is no formal contract, so the rules note acts as the "rules of the game" (p. 57). It should state how a rule change from the academic lead is raised, approved by the ICT sponsor and re-tested. Working with the sponsor and the academic lead is relational. Vendor systems underneath are transactional.
- Reporting should copy the alliance registers (pp. 415-416). Each issue gets logged and closed promptly (p. 302). Accepted workbooks and access tests then act as the measures that contract administration needs (p. 60).

### 4. From contract numbers to controlled change

**Citation:** Rubin, O. (2016). *Control engineering in development projects*. Artech House.

**Purpose:** Rubin follows one motor-stabiliser project through its phases. The maths is only the vehicle. The lesson is how a contract figure becomes a specification, a design, a test and, when things go wrong, a decision.

**Themes:**

1. **Ch2 - Know the hardware and lock down change.** Real parts drift from the datasheet because of tolerances, temperature and age (pp. 17-18). Tests must run at system and subsystem level (p. 18). Software makes change easy, so "the temptation to introduce undisciplined software changes must be avoided" (p. 19).

2. **Ch4 - The contract sets the numbers.** Control requirements must be quantified into specifications in the development contract (p. 52). They come from error analysis, the customer or marketing (pp. 52-53). Do a preliminary design before agreeing, and keep a margin of reserve, since missing the specification has contractual and financial implications (p. 53). With no hardware yet, the team relies on simulation, and early mistakes are costly later (p. 51). Unknowns go into a measurement programme in the technical plan (p. 65).

3. **Ch5 - Design in layers, verify the whole.** The design phase keeps using the concept-phase model (p. 69). The inner current loop is designed first, then the outer rate loop (pp. 95-96). Final verification is best done on the full simulation (p. 96). Control engineers should state their requirements to the hardware designers (pp. 97-98).

4. **Ch6 - Integrate in small steps and expect setbacks.** The design already used a reduced gain to allow for unknowns (p. 101). Loops are closed one at a time, with small gains raised slowly to design values and polarity checked first (pp. 101-102). Even so, the system oscillated at a gain of 30 against a design value of 400 (p. 102). The team agreed to fix the model before the controller, with the whole project team involved (p. 104).

5. **Cross-cutting - Change has a cost and an owner.** A new sensor means altered drawings, new money and new plans. Even a software-only fix carries real cost (p. 118). The engineer would not assume stiffer linkages without a go-ahead from the mechanical designers (p. 117). The last resort is to renegotiate the contract for reduced performance (p. 122).

6. **Cross-cutting - Evidence settles arguments.** Operating trials verify the product meets its specification. Sometimes the client accepts simulated results as a proxy. When performance falls short, simulation can resolve conflict within the team (p. 123).

Ch9 (nuclear power plants) is not on the module list. It applies the same simulation-led approach to a large, safety-critical process plant (ch. 9).

**Key takeaways for ITW601:**
- The rules note is my contract specification. Each rule needs a testable number agreed with the academic lead before I build. The zero-difference check against the accepted workbook is my acceptance test. Building against fictional data first is my simulation before hardware.
- Integrate like Rubin closes loops. A conservative read-only release is my reduced gain. Server-side access tests and the Year 12 unchanged check confirm each layer before the next one goes live.
- Every Year 11 rule change goes to the academic lead, who owns the rules, and independent review gates the merge. If a target cannot be met, I take a scoped trade-off to the ICT sponsor early, the way Rubin treats renegotiation.

### 5. Specifications, baselines, design review and change control

**Citation:** Blanchard, B. S., & Blyler, J. E. (2016). *System engineering management* (5th ed.). Wiley.

**Purpose:** Shows how a stated need becomes a specification, how design is checked against it, and who holds the authority to change it. Chapter 6 (the SEMP) was not supplied, and the ProQuest extracts print no book page numbers, so locators below are section numbers (s.).

**Themes:**

1. **Ch2 - Define the problem before the solution.** The process starts from a deficiency described "in specific qualitative and quantitative terms" (s. 2.1). The authors warn that software teams often code before the functional need is clear. Needs analysis settles what is required before committing to how (s. 2.2).

2. **Ch2 - Measurable, allocated, traceable requirements.** Design cannot proceed well without measurable goals, so priorities are set as technical performance measures (TPMs) in the system specification (s. 2.6). Requirements are then allocated down to components and traced both ways through a specification tree: Type A for the system, Types B to E below it (s. 2.8.2-2.8.3).

3. **Ch2 - Evaluate, correct, then control change.** Test results are compared with the initially specified requirements, and any gap feeds a corrective-action loop (s. 2.11.4). Two of the five post-test questions ask whether the system meets its TPMs and whether it meets all consumer requirements. In effect this is the verification and validation split. Every change is assessed for system-wide impact before approval (s. 2.11.5).

4. **Ch5 - Informal and formal review work together.** Day-to-day review keeps everyone on one baseline and catches conflicts early. Skipping it to save time pushes problems into later, costlier testing (s. 5.2). Formal reviews add a chaired board, an agenda, and action items with named owners and time limits. They also leave "a formal report (record) of major design decisions" (s. 5.3).

5. **Ch5 - Baselines and change control.** Requirements give a functional baseline, allocation an allocated baseline, and detail design a product baseline (Ch5 intro). Once set, changes must be tightly controlled through configuration management (s. 5.4). Each engineering change proposal states the problem, alternatives, impacts and cost, then goes to a change control board. Class 1 changes touch form, fit or function; Class 2 changes do not.

6. **Ch7 - Responsibility must come with authority.** Responsibility for system engineering, "along with the authority", must be clearly defined from the beginning (s. 7.2). A customer who delegates must also hand over input data and leeway to decide, and the statement of work should say what and when, not how (s. 7.3). Matrix structures share authority but can leave staff caught between two bosses (s. 7.4.3).

| Formal review | When | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| Conceptual (system requirements) | End of conceptual design | Functional baseline, Type A specification |
| System design | Preliminary design | Allocation, Types B to E specifications |
| Equipment/software | Detail design | Item design, Types C to E specifications |
| Critical | Before release to production | Product baseline, design "frozen" |

Source: s. 5.3.1-5.3.4.

**Key takeaways for ITW601:**
- The rules note is our de facto Type A specification and the accepted workbooks are our functional baseline. R1-R4 work like small TPMs because each is measurable. A rule change after acceptance goes in the control journal as a mini change proposal - problem, impact, decision.
- The R1 zero-difference test is verification against the specified requirement. The academic lead using the page and calling it fair is validation against the real need. The two test questions in s. 2.11.4 draw the same line.
- Independent review before every merge is our informal day-to-day review. The separate recorded decision to enable anything for staff plays the critical-review role: a formal release point with a record. Responsibility and authority stay paired - the academic lead owns the awards rules, the ICT sponsor sponsors the work.

## Activity 1 video: verification and validation (de Weck, MIT OCW, 2017)

De Weck places V&V on the right side of the V-model, after stakeholder analysis, requirements with target values, and design. Then he closes **two loops** (00:04:23-00:06:00):

| | Verification (inner loop) | Validation (outer loop) |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Did we build it right? Does the design as implemented satisfy the requirements as written? | Did we build the right thing? Does it meet the stakeholders' original intent? |
| When | During development | During or after integration |
| Where | Lab or test stand, controlled conditions | Real or simulated mission environment |
| Scope | Components and subsystems | The full-up system (or dummies standing in) |
| Main method | Testing, among others | Stakeholders try it in realistic use (the CONOPS) |

"It is possible that you satisfy the requirements, but not the stakeholders" (00:04:18). The two words are not synonyms.

**For ITW601:**
- **Verification:** R1, zero differences against the accepted workbook, and the server-side access test for each role. Both run in a controlled environment against the requirement as written.
- **Validation:** the academic lead and Operations staff using the page with real rounds and calling the ranking fair. That cannot be done on one component; it needs the whole page.
